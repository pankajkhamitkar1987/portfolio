Act 01 / Context

A fragmented ecosystem serving thousands of cardholders daily

Fiserv's card servicing suite was built over decades: five separate applications, each developed independently, each requiring different institutional knowledge to navigate. Customer Support Representatives at Financial Institutions juggled these systems simultaneously to handle a single cardholder query.

The problem was not that the systems were broken. It was that they were never designed to work together. Every workaround a rep built was knowledge that lived in their head, not in the product.

01
Application sprawl
Reps toggled between Transaction Journal, Card Management, Dispute Manager, Case Tracker, and Credit Console to resolve a single call.
02
Institutionalised knowledge
New staff could not function without months of shadowing. The system rewarded tenure, not good design.
03
Debit and credit as separate worlds
Financial Institutions wanted a single cardholder view. The existing architecture made that structurally impossible.
04
Workarounds as standard practice
Most FI clients had built their own navigation hacks to make the system workable. The product had normalised friction.
The brief
Replace Card Management and Credit Console with a single unified web portal. Bring debit and credit into one cardholder view. Design for the support rep, not the database schema.
Scale of engagement
80+ Globant contributors across design, engineering, QA and strategy. 24+ Financial Institutions engaged. 48+ sprints across a two-year build and delivery cycle.

Act 02 / My Role

India-side UX anchor in a distributed design chain

I joined the project after the discovery phase was complete. The research foundation, stakeholder interviews across 18 US states, and FI co-creation sessions were already done. My job was to translate that research into design that actually worked, and to keep the design moving.

The design team was distributed across the USA, Mexico, and India. Researchers and the Design Lead operated from the US. Two UX Designers and two Visual Designers worked from Mexico. I was the sole UX Designer from India, working alongside one Visual Designer.

This was not a team that could rely on spontaneous collaboration. We ran a deliberate handoff chain: I completed design work in Indian Standard Time, handed to the Mexico team as their day began, and they continued through to the US team's working hours. Design did not stop. The model required each person to leave work that was specific enough to continue, and documented enough to not lose context.

My direct contributions
Interaction design across multiple workflows. Structural definition of the dual-tab navigation model that unified debit and credit into a single cardholder view. Close coordination with the India-based development team from design handoff through to production release, remaining one of the last design team members on the engagement.

The navigation pivot

The most significant structural challenge was how to unify debit and credit in a single interface. Fiserv's stakeholder feedback pushed toward a dense, information-rich layout where everything was visible upfront. User research pointed the other direction: cognitive overload was already destroying efficiency in the legacy system. Adding density was not the answer.

Three architectural approaches were formally evaluated with clients. The eventual direction retained progressive disclosure but eliminated the need to navigate to a separate detail page for primary actions. The dual-tab model assigned each card type its most relevant primary view by default: Credit defaulted to the Account tab, Debit defaulted to the Card tab. This was not aesthetic preference. It was grounded in a specific insight: credit and debit users have fundamentally different primary tasks when a cardholder calls in.

Navigation architecture comparison: Options A, B, C
Design exploration comparing three information architectures in response to Fiserv stakeholder feedback on density and navigation simplification.
What Fiserv stakeholders asked for
A "stock trader" density model. Everything visible. No clicks to reach key data. Maximum information upfront, space filled 100%.
What users actually needed
Progressive disclosure. Hierarchy that matched task frequency. Quick access to the most critical information with deeper data one step away, not buried in scroll.
Design rationale
The dual-tab default model resolved the tension without compromising either goal. Stakeholders got a dense, data-rich overview. Users got a system where the most relevant information for their card type was immediately visible without hunting. Credit and debit were unified in one cardholder view while retaining the task-appropriate defaults each required.

Act 04 / Solution

One cardholder view. Every card function.

Card Console replaced five applications with a single portal. The cardholder profile persisted across every interaction. Navigation was anchored to the cardholder, not to the function being performed. Support reps could move between card management, transaction history, dispute initiation, and account modification without losing context.

Key design principles that held across the engagement

Progressive disclosure
Primary information upfront. Detail on demand. Rarely-used data never competed with the critical path for attention.
Contextual actions
Actions surfaced near the data they acted on. No hunting through menus. The interface guided without removing user control.
Cardholder as anchor
Navigation organised around the person being served, not the function being performed. The cardholder profile persisted across every interaction state.
Reduced training burden
The system was designed to be learnable without institutional knowledge. New reps could orient themselves through the interface rather than through colleague instruction.

Act 05 / Reflection

What this engagement taught me

Card Console was the most operationally demanding project I had worked on to that point. Two years of active delivery, a team distributed across three countries, weekly client-facing design reviews, and a development partnership that lasted through to production. The lessons were not all about UX craft.

Distributed design is a discipline
A handoff chain only works when every person in it leaves work that is complete enough to continue and documented enough to not lose context. I learned to design for handoff as a first-class output, not an afterthought.
Stakeholder feedback is data, not direction
Fiserv's "stock trader density" request was a signal about what they valued, not a specification to execute. Reading the intent behind the request and responding with evidence from user research is where the real design work happened.
Joining mid-stream is a skill
I did not run the discovery. I inherited it. Learning to build genuine ownership of a design direction you did not originate, and to make meaningful contributions within that foundation, is a capability that most projects eventually demand.
Implementation proximity matters
Staying through to production release and working directly with the engineering team gave me a view of where design intent survived the build and where it did not. That feedback loop changed how I write specifications.

The Problem

Hundreds of games.
No shared language to understand them.

Endeavour Group's gaming portfolio contained hundreds of slot machines across 350+ venues. Each venue was making decisions about what to buy, what to replace, and what to keep. But there was no consistent framework for how games were compared or discussed.

Games were referred to by manufacturer names, individual titles, or personal familiarity. When a game underperformed, there was no structured way to understand why, or which category of game might replace it for a specific player audience.

The original assumption was straightforward: categorise games using their mathematical attributes. Hit Rate. Volatility. Feature Frequency. RTP. These are the industry-standard levers.

That assumption did not survive contact with reality.

The ideal data and the available data were not the same thing. A framework built on what you cannot access is not a framework. It is a liability.

Framework Evolution

Three iterations.
One fundamental shift in thinking.

Phase 01  •  Abandoned

Math-Driven Classification

Games grouped by measurable mathematical behaviour. Theoretically sound, practically impossible. The data that would make this model credible was not publicly available from any manufacturer.

Hit Rate Volatility Feature Frequency RTP Revenue
Phase 02  •  Transitional

Feature-Driven Exploration

Shifted to observable game characteristics. Better, but created a new problem: the inventory of mechanics became enormous. Every Hold and Spin variant threatened to become its own category.

Main Features Free Game Mechanisms Graphics Style Theme
Phase 03  •  Final

Engagement-Driven Clustering

Instead of cataloguing mechanics, the model asked: what experience does this create for the player? Features were grouped into clusters based on the emotion and engagement pattern they produce.

The Shift That Made It Work

Categorisation becomes more useful when it focuses on player experience rather than hidden technical characteristics.

This reframe transformed the project from a data classification exercise into a player-centric decision-making tool. The same shift that made GIH useful at the platform level, applied here at the knowledge layer beneath it.

Key Decisions

Nine decisions that
shaped the final framework

01
Decision
Abandon mathematical categorisation entirely

Manufacturer data was not consistently available. Estimation-based classification created inconsistency and low stakeholder confidence. A framework built on assumed data is not trustworthy at scale.

Use attributes that can be reliably collected. Observable beats theoretically ideal.
02
Decision
Categorise by engagement, not by mechanics

Even with full data, stakeholders cared more about why players choose games and how those games feel than about internal probability structures. Player experience was the more useful differentiator.

Player experience is often a stronger differentiator than hidden game mathematics.
03
Decision
Hold and Spin becomes a Feature Category, not a Game Category

A significant proportion of the modern Australian pub portfolio uses Hold and Spin. Using it as a category would collapse the majority of games into one bucket, making the framework analytically useless.

A mechanic can be too common to function as a useful category. Categories must differentiate, not just describe.
04
Decision
Create Hold and Spin subtypes based on experience

Not all Hold and Spin experiences feel the same. Simple collection mechanics sit in a fundamentally different player experience to theatrical, layered, unlock-driven variants.

The implementation of a mechanic is often more important than the mechanic itself.
05
Decision
Jackpot becomes metadata, not a primary category

Dragon Link and Dragon Cash share almost identical core gameplay. The difference is configuration: linked progressive vs standalone. The game category should not change based on how the prize structure is configured.

Not every important attribute should become a category. Some attributes are better treated as metadata.
06
Decision
Introduce feature clustering to manage inventory scale

The full inventory of game mechanics was enormous. Treating each as its own category created excessive complexity with no analytical benefit. Affinity-mapping techniques from UX research were applied to group mechanics by the experience they create.

Clustering simplifies complexity without losing meaning.
07
Decision
Accept hybrid features with primary and secondary categories

Modern games combine mechanics. Forcing a game into a single category when it genuinely belongs to two creates artificial limitations that weaken the model over time.

Modern game design is increasingly hybrid. Frameworks should accommodate overlap rather than eliminate it.
08
Decision
Retain both Engagement and Business category perspectives

Gaming analysts thought in terms of engagement style. Purchasing teams thought in terms of operational performance categories. Both views were valid. Neither could be discarded.

Different stakeholders require different lenses. A framework becomes stronger when it supports multiple viewpoints.
09
Decision
Separate player motivations into archetypes, not game subcategories

Jackpot Chasing is a player motivation, not a game type. Confusing what a game does with why someone chooses it created definitional problems throughout early iterations.

Player behaviour and game design should be connected but not confused. They are different layers of the same model.

Reflection

What this project proves
about design under constraint

Learning 01

Constraints produce better frameworks than freedom

The inability to access manufacturer math data forced the project toward a more useful model. A framework grounded in observable, player-centric attributes is more maintainable, more scalable, and more comprehensible to stakeholders than one dependent on hidden mathematical inputs. The constraint was the design brief.

Learning 02

Information architecture is strategy work

Taxonomy design at this level is not a documentation exercise. The decisions about what becomes a category, what becomes metadata, and what becomes a separate layer directly determine what business questions the framework can and cannot answer. Every IA decision is a business capability decision.

Learning 03

Stakeholder feedback is design input, not design noise

Three of the nine key decisions in this project were triggered directly by stakeholder questions. When a domain expert asks a pointed question, that is not pushback. That is a design brief arriving through conversation. The ability to hear it as such is a design skill.

Learning 04

Value does not require shipping

This framework was not fully integrated into a live product during the engagement. The intellectual work, the decisions, the research, and the documented model still represent a strategic asset for the organisation. Design leadership means delivering value at the thinking level, not only at the deployment level.
